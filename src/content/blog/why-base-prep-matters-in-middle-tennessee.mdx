Most concrete failures don't start with the concrete itself — they start with what's underneath it. In Middle Tennessee, that usually means one of two things: soil that hasn't been compacted correctly, or a base that can't handle how much the ground moves through the seasons.

## Clay soil expands and contracts

A lot of the soil around Nashville has a meaningful clay content, which means it holds water and shifts more than sandier soils. As it absorbs moisture it expands; as it dries out, it contracts. A slab poured directly on unstable or poorly compacted native soil will move with it — and concrete doesn't flex, it cracks.

## Freeze-thaw cycles add another layer

Middle Tennessee doesn't see the extreme winters of the upper Midwest, but it does see repeated freeze-thaw swings through the winter months. Water that gets into the base layer and freezes expands, pushing up on the slab above it. Over enough cycles, that repeated movement telegraphs through to the surface.

## What proper base prep actually looks like

- **Removing organic material.** Topsoil and roots decompose over time, leaving voids under the slab.
- **Compacting in lifts.** A gravel base compacted in stages holds up far better than one dumped and never machine-compacted.
- **Grading for drainage.** Water that can't get away from the slab is water that will eventually find its way underneath it.
- **Correct control joint spacing.** Even a well-prepared slab needs joints placed to control where it cracks as it cures and ages.

None of this is visible once the job is finished, which is exactly why it's worth asking a contractor how they approach it before signing off on a quote.
